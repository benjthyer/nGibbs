\begin{abstract}
This note describes how the continuous-saturation emulator (\code{ContinuousModel}) is
trained, with an emphasis on how each term of the loss is evaluated on a mini-batch. The
model is defined in \code{NN_continuous.py} and trained by \code{train_Upper_MELTS} in
\code{trainer.py}. Table~\ref{tab:key} is the key to all symbols.
Section~\ref{sec:affinity} describes the affinity-aware subclass, \code{AffinityModel},
and only where it differs.
\end{abstract}

%=====================================================================================

\section{The forward model}
%=====================================================================================

The network sees $[u, b]$. An encoder produces a feature vector $h$, which is concatenated
with the raw input. From it:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item one softmax head per variable phase gives that phase's endmember fractions $\hat x$.
        Endmembers containing an element that is absent from $b$ are given zero probability;
  \item one small network per phase, which also sees $\hat x$, gives the affinity $g$;
  \item the abundance is the rectified affinity,
  \begin{equation}
    \hat n = \max(g, 0).
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Because $\max(g,0)$ is continuous at $g=0$, a phase leaving the assemblage is a kink in
$\hat n$, not a jump. (In the code $g$ first passes through a leaky ReLU and is then
clamped at zero; the result is identical.)

Component moles and the reconstructed bulk follow from stoichiometry:
\begin{align}
  \hat m &= (\hat x V + f) \odot (\hat n M), \label{eq:m}\\
  \hat b &= \frac{\hat m A}{\sum_e (\hat m A)_e}. \label{eq:bhat}
\end{align}
Here $\odot$ is the element-wise product, so $\hat m$ is each component's phase abundance
times its fraction in that phase. Mass balance (adjusting $\hat m$ until $\hat b = b$
exactly) is applied only at inference, by \code{NN_MELTS.apply_mass_balance}, and is not
part of the trained network.

%=====================================================================================
\section{The training objective}
%=====================================================================================

For each mini-batch the trainer minimises
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
  L = 10^4\big(\lambda_s L_s + \lambda_x L_x + \lambda_n L_n + \lambda_b L_b\big)
\;}
\label{eq:total}
\end{equation}
covering phase presence ($L_s$), chemistry ($L_x$), phase abundance ($L_n$) and bulk
($L_b$). The factor $10^4$ keeps the loss large compared with the optimiser's $\epsilon$.
The bulk term is added only when $\lambda_b \neq 0$. Typical recipe values are
$\lambda_x = 500$, $\lambda_n = 50$, $\lambda_s$ between $0.002$ and $0.02$, and
$\lambda_b = 0$ while tuning, rising to $50$ for the final joint training.
The same function (\code{_upper_loss}) computes the training loss and the validation
loss, so a model is always scored on the objective it was trained on.

In every term below, sums ($\sum$) run over all B rows of the batch as well as over the
index shown, and each term is a weighted mean: the weighted sum of errors divided by the
sum of the weights.

\subsection{The error function $\rho$}
Chemistry, abundance and bulk use the symmetric relative squared error
(\code{symmetric_rel_l2}),
\begin{equation}
  \rho(\hat y, y) = \frac{(\hat y - y)^2}{|\hat y| + |y|},
  \label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
with the denominator floored at $10^{-6}$. The type is chosen separately for each term by
\code{loss_config.<term>.type} (\code{symmetric_rel_l1} and \code{huber} are also
available). Two limits explain its behaviour:
\begin{align}
  y = 0:\qquad & \rho(\hat y, 0) = |\hat y|, \label{eq:rho0}\\
  \hat y \approx y:\qquad & \rho(\hat y, y) \approx \frac{(\hat y - y)^2}{2y}. \label{eq:rhorel}
\end{align}
For a target of zero it acts like an absolute ($L_1$) error, a constant pull toward zero.
Near a non-zero target it is a squared error divided by the target, so a small abundance
counts as much as a large one in relative terms.

\subsection{Chemistry loss $L_x$}
Only phases that are present in the ground truth have a composition, so the predicted
fractions are masked by $\bar s$ before comparison and the mean runs over present
endmembers only:
\begin{equation}
  L_x = \frac{\sum_k \bar s_k\, \omega_k\, \rho(\bar s_k \hat x_k,\ x_k)}{\sum_k \bar s_k\, \omega_k},
  \qquad k = 1,\dots,\text{VC}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Abundance loss $L_n$}
Every phase is supervised, including absent ones (target $n=0$): learning where an
abundance reaches zero is the point of the continuous model. The loss is not computed on
$\hat n$ itself but on a smoothed version of it controlled by the boundary temperature
$T$ (Section~\ref{sec:anneal}):
\begin{equation}
  \tilde n = T \softplus(g/T) = T \ln\!\big(1 + e^{g/T}\big),
  \label{eq:smooth}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  L_n = \frac{\sum_j w_j\, \rho(\tilde n_j, n_j)}{\sum_j w_j},
  \qquad j = 1,\dots,\text{P}.
\end{equation}
$\tilde n$ is a smooth stand-in for $\max(g,0)$ with two useful properties:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\tilde n}{dg} = \operatorname{sigmoid}(g/T),
  \qquad
  0 < \tilde n - \max(g,0) \le T \ln 2 .
  \label{eq:smoothprops}
\end{equation}
The error relative to $\hat n$ is largest ($T\ln 2$) at $g = 0$, negligible once
$|g| > 3T$, and shrinks as $T$ is annealed. For an absent phase, Eq.~\eqref{eq:rho0} gives
$\rho(\tilde n, 0) = \tilde n$, so the gradient on its affinity is
$\operatorname{sigmoid}(g/T)$. This pushes $g$ negative and fades to zero once the phase
is confidently absent ($g \ll -T$), instead of holding it at the boundary $g=0$.

\subsection{Presence loss $L_s$}
There is no separate presence head. Instead the affinity is also used as the logit of a
binary cross-entropy against $s$:
\begin{equation}
  L_s = \frac{\sum_j c_j\, \BCE\!\big(5g_j/T_{0,j},\ s_j\big)}{\sum_j c_j},
  \qquad
  c = \big(s\,w + (1-s)\big)\,\big|\tanh(g/T)\big|,
  \label{eq:sat}
\end{equation}
with $\BCE(z,s) = -s\ln\operatorname{sigmoid}(z) - (1-s)\ln\big(1-\operatorname{sigmoid}(z)\big)$.
Three choices are built into Eq.~\eqref{eq:sat}:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Phase weights apply only to the present examples ($s=1$). Those are the examples a
        rare phase is short of; absent examples keep weight 1.
  \item The logit is scaled by the fixed $T_0$, not the annealed $T$, so the classifier's
        confidence means the same thing throughout training: it reaches
        $\operatorname{sigmoid}(5) \approx 0.99$ at $g = T_0$.
  \item The weight $|\tanh(g/T)|$ uses the annealed $T$. It is zero at the boundary and
        close to 1 a few $T$ away. It hands off with the abundance loss, whose gradient
        $\operatorname{sigmoid}(g/T)$ is largest near the boundary and fades far from it:
        $L_n$ handles calibration close to $g=0$, and $L_s$ corrects samples that are
        confidently wrong far from it.
\end{enumerate}
The weight $c$ depends on $g$ and is not detached, so it contributes to the gradient.

\subsection{Bulk loss $L_b$}
The reconstructed bulk (Eq.~\ref{eq:bhat}) is compared with the input bulk over the
elements present in it:
\begin{equation}
  L_b = \frac{\sum_{e:\, b_e > 0} \rho(\hat b_e, b_e)}{\sum_{e:\, b_e > 0} 1},
  \qquad e = 1,\dots,\text{E}.
\end{equation}
This measures how far the raw predictions are from satisfying mass balance, which reduces
the correction that has to be applied at inference. It is meaningless until the mole heads
have been trained, so it is switched on only for the final joint training. $\hat b$ is
built from the unsmoothed $\hat n$.

%=====================================================================================
\section{Boundary temperature}
\label{sec:anneal}
%=====================================================================================

\paragraph{The reference scale $T_0$.}
$T_0$ sets, for each phase, how small an abundance is ``small''. It is the 5th percentile
of that phase's abundance over the rows in which the phase is present:
\begin{equation}
  T_{0,j} = 5\text{th percentile of } \{\, n_j : n_j > 0 \,\}.
\end{equation}
An abundance below $T_{0,j}$ is therefore smaller than in 95\% of the rows where phase $j$
is present. Because the training and validation sets are generated the same way, their
$T_0$ values are essentially identical, so $T_0$ is computed on the smaller validation set,
where it is cheaper. Across a typical dataset $T_0$ spans about three orders of magnitude
(roughly $10^{-5}$ for trace phases to $0.06$ for dominant ones), which is why one
temperature for all phases would not work. A phase that never occurs gets $T_0 = 0$; for
the division in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:smooth}--\eqref{eq:sat} it is replaced by $10^{-6}$ times
the smallest non-zero $T_0$, which makes its smoothing negligible.

\paragraph{Annealing.}
During an episode of $N$ epochs, the temperature is $T = a\,T_0$, with $a$ decreasing
linearly from $a_{\text{start}}$ to $a_{\text{end}}$:
\begin{equation}
  a = a_{\text{start}} + (a_{\text{end}} - a_{\text{start}})\,\frac{\text{epoch}}{N-1},
  \qquad \text{epoch} = 0, \dots, N-1.
\end{equation}
Early in training a large $T$ gives every phase a wide, smooth transition region and
well-behaved gradients. As $T$ shrinks, $\tilde n$ approaches $\hat n = \max(g,0)$ and the
model is trained on its actual output. The schedule restarts in every episode that enables
it (\code{boundary_temperature} in the recipe). Recipes typically chain two episodes,
$a: 1 \to 0.05$ and then $a: 0.05 \to 10^{-4}$. All arithmetic involving $g/T$, and the mole
heads that produce $g$, run in 32-bit floats even under mixed precision, because late in
the schedule $g/T$ would overflow or lose precision in 16-bit formats.

%=====================================================================================
\section{The training loop}
%=====================================================================================

\paragraph{Episodes.}
A recipe is a sequence of episodes, each with its own number of epochs, learning-rate
schedule, frozen modules and loss-weight overrides. The continuous recipes run:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item tune the encoder and chemistry heads, with the mole heads frozen and
        $\lambda_n = \lambda_s = 0$;
  \item tune the mole heads, with the encoder and chemistry heads frozen and
        $\lambda_x = 0$;
  \item train everything jointly, with annealing on and $\lambda_b > 0$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Optimiser and input noise.}
AdamW (with AMSGrad and $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$), with weight decay set per module: encoder
\code{lowWD}, chemistry heads \code{highWD}, mole heads none. Training inputs can be
perturbed with multiplicative Gaussian noise, $[u,b] \leftarrow [u,b]\,(1 + \sigma\xi)$
with $\xi \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$; the perturbed $b$ is also the bulk target in $L_b$.

\paragraph{Validation, checkpointing and early stopping.}
Before the first epoch, the incoming model is scored on the validation set; that score is
the one to beat. After each epoch the validation loss (Eq.~\ref{eq:total}, at that epoch's
$T$) is computed, and the model is saved whenever it improves on the best so far. Training
stops after \code{early_stopping_patience} epochs without improvement, and the best saved
model is restored.

\paragraph{Adaptive regularisation.}
After each epoch, if the validation loss exceeds 1.02 times the training loss, the
dropout rate (outside the mole heads) is raised one step up to its ceiling; once at the
ceiling, the input noise $\sigma$ is raised instead. If the validation loss is below the
training loss, dropout (or, once dropout is zero, $\sigma$) is lowered one step.

\paragraph{Remark on checkpoint selection under annealing.}
The validation loss is evaluated at a different $T$ each epoch. Both the smoothing error
($\le T\ln 2$) and $\tilde n$ for absent phases shrink with $T$, so the validation loss
tends to fall as the schedule progresses regardless of model quality. Checkpoint
selection and early stopping therefore favour later epochs, and the starting score
(computed at the first epoch's $T$) is not directly comparable with later ones.

\section{The affinity-aware model (AffinityModel)}
\label{sec:affinity}
%=====================================================================================

\code{AffinityModel} (\code{NN_continuous.py}) is a subclass of \code{ContinuousModel}.
The architecture, chemistry loss $L_x$, bulk loss $L_b$, phase weights and training loop
are unchanged. Three things differ:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item the abundance target: absent phases are labelled with how far they are from
        saturating, an estimate of their affinity, instead of with zero;
  \item the mole-head activation and the abundance loss $L_n$, which replace the softplus
        smoothing;
  \item there is no presence loss ($L_s$, $\lambda_s = 0$), no $T_0$ and no annealing:
        the sign of the prediction is the presence call.
\end{enumerate}
Because there is no annealing, the boundary temperature of Section~\ref{sec:anneal} plays
no role here, and in this section $T$ denotes temperature. Table~\ref{tab:affkey} lists the
symbols this section adds to Table~\ref{tab:key}.

\subsection{Estimating affinities from $dn/dT$ (MELTS)}

The thermodynamic models report abundances, not affinities, so the affinity of an absent
phase is estimated by extrapolating its abundance trend past the point where it saturates.
The estimate is first order. Near a saturation boundary, at fixed pressure and bulk, both the
abundance on the stable side and the affinity on the unstable side vary linearly with the
distance $|T - T_{\rm sat}|$, so the extrapolated abundance is proportional to the affinity
close to the boundary.

\paragraph{Runs and segments.}
The MELTS training data are isobaric cooling runs, either batch (constant bulk) or
fractional crystallisation (FxTal, crystals removed at every step). Labelling needs the rows
in simulation order, so it runs on the exported arrays before the training set is shuffled,
using a run identifier exported with each row (\code{run_ids}). Each run is split into
segments along which $T$ moves monotonically. A segment is treated as FxTal if any of its
bulk element fractions varies by more than $10^{-3}$.

\paragraph{Crossing slope.}
A crossing is a pair of adjacent rows in one segment where phase $j$ changes from present
to absent. Let $p$ be the present row, $a$ the absent row, and $p'$ the next row past $p$,
away from $a$ (also present). The slope $r = |dn/dT|$ at the crossing is
\begin{align}
  \text{batch:}\quad & r = \frac{|n_{p'} - n_p|}{|T_{p'} - T_p|}, \label{eq:rbatch}\\
  \text{FxTal:}\quad & r = \frac{n_{p'}}{|T_{p'} - T_p|}, \label{eq:rfx}
\end{align}
for phase $j$. In an FxTal run the solids of each step are removed, so the abundance at a
fully saturated step is already the amount crystallised over that step; in a batch run it
is the difference between steps. If $p'$ is absent or outside the segment, or the estimate is not positive,
the fallback $r = n_p / |T_a - T_p|$ is used. The saturation temperature is where the line
through $(T_p, n_p)$ with slope $r$ reaches zero, toward $a$:
\begin{equation}
  T_{\rm sat} = T_p \pm \frac{n_p}{r},
\end{equation}
clamped to lie between $T_p$ and $T_a$.

\paragraph{Labels.}
For each row and phase,
\begin{equation}
  g = \begin{cases}
    n, & \text{phase present},\\
    -r\,|T - T_{\rm sat}|, & \text{phase absent},\\
    \text{undefined (NaN)}, & \text{phase never present in the segment},
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:glabel}
\end{equation}
where an absent row uses the crossing in its segment whose $T_{\rm sat}$ is nearest to its
$T$, and $r$ is that crossing's slope. Both $g$ and $r$ are stored with the bundle.
Because $g = n$ wherever the phase is present, $\max(g,0) = n$ everywhere, and $g$ passes
through zero (approximately) at $T_{\rm sat}$.

\paragraph{Per-phase statistics.}
From all crossings in the validation set (which is cheaper and statistically equivalent to
the training set, as for $T_0$), each phase gets
\begin{equation}
  c = \Delta \cdot 10^{\operatorname{median}(\log_{10} r)}, \qquad \Delta = 25~\text{K},
  \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}
the abundance that a typical crossing of that phase accumulates over 25~K. The mean $\mu$
and standard deviation $\sigma$ of $\log_{10} r$ are computed after dropping the 5\% of
crossings farthest from the median. The median is used rather than the mean because slopes
are close to log-normally distributed with a heavy tail. A phase with fewer than 20
validation crossings is \emph{unreliable}: its $c$ is replaced by $10^{\operatorname{median}(\log_{10} c)}$ over the
reliable phases, and all of its absent cells are untrusted (below).

\paragraph{HeFESTo.}
The projection is not implemented for HeFESTo. Its transects do not hold entropy constant,
so the extrapolation above does not apply. HeFESTo does report true affinities, and how to
scale them into targets is left for later work.

\subsection{Targets and trust mask}

The labels are converted into training targets when the training workspace is built. The
label is floored at $-3c$ and passed through the activation
\begin{equation}
  h(g) = \begin{cases} g, & g \ge 0,\\ c\tanh(g/c) + \varepsilon g, & g < 0, \end{cases}
  \qquad \varepsilon = 0.02,
  \label{eq:h}
\end{equation}
which leaves present abundances unchanged and compresses the absent side toward $-c$.
For a phase with a typical slope, a distance $\Delta$ from saturation gives $g = -c$ and
$h(-c) \approx -0.78c$. The map is close to linear at smaller distances. Beyond that the
targets level off (to $h(-3c) \approx -1.06c$) but keep their order through the
$\varepsilon g$ term. This stops the far-field extrapolations, where the first-order estimate
is weakest, from dominating the loss.

A cell is \emph{trusted} ($\tau = 1$) if the phase is present, or if it is absent with a
defined label and a slope that is not anomalously shallow:
\begin{equation}
  \tau = 1 \iff g > 0 \ \ \text{or}\ \ \big(g \le 0,\ g,r \text{ defined},\ r \ge r_{\min}\big),
  \qquad r_{\min} = 10^{\mu - 2\max(\sigma,\,0.25)}.
\end{equation}
Only the shallow side is filtered: a very small $r$ makes a phase look barely undersaturated
far from its boundary. The targets are
\begin{equation}
  y = \begin{cases} h\big(\max(g, -3c)\big), & \tau = 1,\\ -c, & \tau = 0. \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For untrusted cells, $-c$ is a placeholder: the phase is known to be absent, but not how far
from saturation it is.

\subsection{Forward model and abundance loss}

The mole head applies the same activation to its output,
\begin{equation}
  \hat y = h(\hat g), \qquad \hat n = \max(\hat y, 0) = \max(\hat g, 0),
\end{equation}
so the abundance, the component moles, the reconstructed bulk and inference-time mass
balance are exactly as in the continuous model. The scale $c$ is stored in the model, so
predictions and targets use the same $h$.

The abundance loss $L_n$ is evaluated in units of $c$. With $u = \hat y/c$ and $t = y/c$,
\begin{equation}
  \ell = \begin{cases}
    (u - t)^2, & \tau = 1,\\[2pt]
    \alpha \max(0,\ u + \gamma)^2 + \beta\,(u - t)^2, & \tau = 0,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:affloss}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  L_n = \frac{\sum_j w_j\, \ell_j}{\sum_j w_j}, \qquad j = 1,\dots,\text{P},
\end{equation}
with the sums running over the batch as in Section~3. For an untrusted cell ($t = -1$), the
hinge term is zero whenever $\hat y \le -\gamma c = -c/2$. The prediction is only required
to be clearly negative, and the weak pull ($\beta = 0.05$) toward $-c$ keeps it from
drifting. Measuring the error in units of $c$ puts every phase on a comparable scale,
whatever its typical abundance. The loss is computed in 32-bit floats.

\subsection{What this changes in training}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item The total objective is Eq.~\eqref{eq:total} with $\lambda_s = 0$ and the $L_n$
        above; $\lambda_n$ (\code{loss_config.moles.weight}) still scales it.
  \item No $T_0$ is computed or needed. The per-phase statistics ($c$, $\mu$, $\sigma$) are
        read from the validation bundle instead.
  \item With no annealing, the objective is the same in every epoch, so the remark on
        checkpoint selection in Section~5 does not apply.
  \item The presence used to mask the chemistry loss is still the ground-truth $s$, and
        $\hat s = [\hat g > 0]$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Legality polish for pyroxene and spinel compositions (MELTS)}
\label{sec:polish}
%=====================================================================================

\subsection{Two component bases}

\paragraph{The MELTS basis.} MELTS describes a pyroxene (seven endmembers) or a spinel
(five endmembers) by the mole fractions $x^{\rm M}$ of its endmembers. The oxide moles of
one mole of the phase are
\begin{equation}
  (\text{oxide moles}) = x^{\rm M} L,
  \label{eq:oxM}
\end{equation}
where each row of $L$ is the oxide formula of one endmember (e.g.\ diopside:
1 CaO, 1 MgO, 2 SiO$_2$). Every entry of $L$ is non-negative, but the fractions
$x^{\rm M}$ themselves are not: in the MELTS solution models a perfectly legal pyroxene or
spinel can have some negative endmember fractions. Only certain combinations of them (the
site occupancies, and hence the oxide amounts) must be non-negative. A softmax cannot
output negative numbers, so it cannot predict $x^{\rm M}$ directly.

\paragraph{The transformed basis.} The ML indexer therefore applies a fixed linear change of
basis, the \code{PxSpTransform} block $T$ of each phase:
\begin{equation}
  x = x^{\rm M}\, T .
  \label{eq:xT}
\end{equation}
Every row of $T$ is non-negative and sums to one, so $\sum x = \sum x^{\rm M} = 1$. $T$ was
built (analytically for pyroxene, numerically for spinel) so that legal compositions give
$x \ge 0$: all of them for pyroxene, almost all of the training data for spinel. The network predicts $\hat x$ in this basis with a
softmax, and the ground truth used in the losses is $x$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:xT}. This is the
$\hat x$ / $x$ of Table~\ref{tab:key}.

To get oxides back, invert Eq.~\eqref{eq:xT} and substitute into Eq.~\eqref{eq:oxM}:
\begin{equation}
  (\text{oxide moles}) = x\, T^{-1} L = x\, Q, \qquad Q = T^{-1} L \ (\code{compToOx}).
  \label{eq:oxQ}
\end{equation}
Each column of $T$ is one \emph{transformed component}, and each row of $Q$ is that
component's oxide formula. Because $T^{-1}$ has negative entries, so does $Q$: most
transformed components are not real endmembers but signed combinations of them, with
negative amounts of some oxides. Table~\ref{tab:Kcomp} lists them.

\paragraph{Names.} Only chromite passes through the spinel transform unchanged (its row and
column of $T$ are both the identity), so it keeps its name. Every other transformed
component is written $K$ with an index:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item pyroxene: $K_C, K_0, \dots, K_5$ (the columns $g_C, g_0, \dots, g_5$ of the transform
        file);
  \item spinel: Chr and $K_0, \dots, K_3$ (the columns $w_0, \dots, w_3$).
\end{itemize}
The fraction of component $K_i$ is written $\hat x_i$ (and $\hat x_C$, $\hat x_{\rm Chr}$).
The same symbols are reused for pyroxene and spinel; the subsection makes clear which is
meant. In the code, a transformed component is indexed by the name of the MELTS endmember
in the same position (e.g.\ \code{'essenite'} for $K_4$), which is why
Table~\ref{tab:Kcomp} also gives that name.

Read along the columns of Table~\ref{tab:Kcomp}, each component mostly carries one element:
in pyroxene, $K_0$ carries Fe$^{2+}$, $K_1$ Ti, $K_2$ Al, $K_3$ Fe$^{3+}$, $K_4$ Na and
$K_5$ Mg; in spinel, $K_1$ carries Fe$^{3+}$, $K_2$ Mg and $K_3$ Ti. The price is that the
same components carry negative amounts of other oxides. For example, the spinel component
$K_2$ contains 20 MgO and $-19$ FeO, so it adds Mg by swapping it for Fe.

\paragraph{Why a valid softmax output can still be illegal.} The transform guarantees that
every legal composition lands in the simplex ($\hat x \ge 0$, $\sum\hat x = 1$), but not
the reverse: the legal compositions fill only part of the simplex. The softmax can return
any point in the simplex. Take a spinel with
\begin{equation}
  (\hat x_{\rm Chr}, \hat x_0, \hat x_1, \hat x_2, \hat x_3) = (0.2,\ 0.6,\ 0.1,\ 0.1,\ 0).
  \label{eq:spex}
\end{equation}
Its FeO is $0.2 + 0.6 + 0.1 - 19 \times 0.1 = -1.0$ mol. A pure MgAl$_2$O$_4$ spinel has
$\hat x_2 = 0.05$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:xT}, with $T$ mapping spinel to $0.95K_0 + 0.05K_2$), so a
small overshoot in $\hat x_2$ is enough to make FeO negative.

\subsection{The correction}

\paragraph{One oxide.} By Eq.~\eqref{eq:oxQ}, the moles of a given oxide are a weighted
sum of the fractions, $\sum_i q_i \hat x_i$, where $q_i$ is that oxide's column of $Q$.
Split the components by the sign of $q_i$ and add up each side:
\begin{equation}
  S_+ = \sum_{q_i > 0} q_i\,\hat x_i, \qquad
  S_- = \sum_{q_i < 0} |q_i|\,\hat x_i, \qquad
  \text{oxide moles} = S_+ - S_- .
\end{equation}
The oxide is negative exactly when $S_- > S_+$. The fix is to make the positive side larger
and the negative side smaller by multiplying every fraction on the positive side by one
factor $a$ and every fraction on the negative side by one factor $b$. Two conditions fix the
two factors:
\begin{align}
  \text{oxide becomes exactly zero:}\quad & a S_+ = b S_-, \label{eq:zero}\\
  \text{fractions still sum to one:}\quad & a P + b N = 1, \label{eq:close}
\end{align}
where $P$ and $N$ are the plain sums of the fractions on each side ($P + N = 1$ before the
correction). From Eq.~\eqref{eq:zero}, $b = a S_+/S_-$; substituting into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:close} gives
\begin{equation}
  a = \frac{S_-}{S_- P + S_+ N}, \qquad
  b = \frac{S_+}{S_- P + S_+ N}.
  \label{eq:ab}
\end{equation}
Since $S_+ < S_-$ and $P + N = 1$, the denominator lies between $S_+$ and $S_-$, so
$a > 1$ and $b < 1$: the positive side grows and the negative side shrinks, as intended.
This rescaling has three useful properties:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item a factor multiplies a non-negative fraction, so no fraction can become negative;
  \item the proportions among components on the same side are unchanged, so as much of the
        prediction as possible is kept;
  \item it stops exactly at the boundary (oxide $= 0$), so the composition is changed no
        more than needed along this path.
\end{itemize}
If the positive side is empty ($P = 0$), the denominator is zero and no rescaling can help;
such rows are left unchanged.

\paragraph{Worked example.} One of the clinopyroxenes produced by the iterative mass balance
on the 102 test set has
$(\hat x_C, \hat x_0, \dots, \hat x_5) = (0.581, 0.028, 0.073, 0.177, 0.005, 0.136, 0)$.
With the MgO column of Table~\ref{tab:Kcomp},
\begin{align*}
  S_+ &= \hat x_C + \hat x_1 + 3\hat x_5 = 0.654, \qquad P = 0.654,\\
  S_- &= 2\hat x_0 + \hat x_2 + \hat x_3 + 4\hat x_4 = 0.782, \qquad N = 0.346,
\end{align*}
so MgO $= -0.128$ mol. Eq.~\eqref{eq:ab} gives $a = 1.060$ and $b = 0.887$. Afterwards both
sides equal $0.693$ (MgO $= 0$), and the fractions sum to $1.060 \times 0.654 + 0.887 \times
0.346 = 1$.

\subsection{Orthopyroxene: CaO $\ge 0$}

\paragraph{$K_5$ cap.} In the transformed basis, $\hat x_5$ of any pure endmember is at most
$1/2$ (the largest value, for clinoenstatite, is $1/2$). Rows with $\hat x_5 > 1/2$ are
first set to $\hat x_5 = 1/2$, and the other six fractions are rescaled in proportion so
that they sum to $1/2$.

\paragraph{CaO.} From Table~\ref{tab:Kcomp},
\begin{equation}
  S_+ = \hat x_C + \hat x_0 + \hat x_1 + \hat x_2 + \hat x_3, \qquad
  S_- = 2\hat x_4 + \hat x_5 .
\end{equation}
All positive coefficients are 1, so $P = S_+$ and $N = \hat x_4 + \hat x_5$, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ab} simplifies to the form used in the code:
\begin{equation}
  b = \frac{1}{3\hat x_4 + 2\hat x_5}, \qquad
  a = \frac{S_-}{S_+\,(3\hat x_4 + 2\hat x_5)} .
\end{equation}
The CaO correction is applied to orthopyroxene only.

\subsection{Clinopyroxene: MgO $\ge 0$}

The network alone does not produce negative MgO in clinopyroxene, but the iterative mass
balance occasionally does. From Table~\ref{tab:Kcomp},
\begin{equation}
  S_+ = \hat x_C + \hat x_1 + 3\hat x_5, \quad P = \hat x_C + \hat x_1 + \hat x_5; \qquad
  S_- = 2\hat x_0 + \hat x_2 + \hat x_3 + 4\hat x_4, \quad N = \hat x_0 + \hat x_2 + \hat x_3 + \hat x_4,
\end{equation}
and $a$, $b$ are given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:ab} (the worked example above). The MgO correction
is applied to clinopyroxene only.

\subsection{Spinel: FeO $\ge 0$ and Al$_2$O$_3$ $\ge 0$}

Spinel has two oxides that can go negative. From Table~\ref{tab:Kcomp},
\begin{align}
  \text{FeO} &= \hat x_{\rm Chr} + \hat x_0 + \hat x_1 + \tfrac94\hat x_3 - 19\,\hat x_2, \label{eq:spFe}\\
  \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 &= \hat x_0 + \hat x_2 - \tfrac23\hat x_1 - \tfrac14\hat x_3 . \label{eq:spAl}
\end{align}
A row is corrected when either is below $-10^{-6}$ mol (smaller values are float32
round-off). In models without Cr, $\hat x_{\rm Chr} = 0$.

\paragraph{Three pools.} Because the two constraints share components, the components are
grouped by the signs they carry in the two equations, and each group gets its own factor:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l c c c@{}}
\toprule
Pool & Components & FeO & Al$_2$O$_3$ & Factor \\
\midrule
$A = \hat x_{\rm Chr} + \hat x_0$ & Chr, $K_0$ & $+$ & $+$ or 0 & $a$ \\
$B = \hat x_1 + \hat x_3$         & $K_1$, $K_3$ & $+$ & $-$ & $b$ \\
$C = \hat x_2$                    & $K_2$ & $-$ & $+$ & $c$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Negative FeO can only be fixed by trading $C$ (the only negative FeO term) against $A$;
negative Al$_2$O$_3$ only by trading $B$ (the negative Al$_2$O$_3$ terms) against $A$. In
every case closure is kept,
\begin{equation}
  aA + bB + cC = A + B + C, \label{eq:spclose}
\end{equation}
and only the violated oxide is set to zero, with the pool it does not involve held fixed:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{FeO only} ($b = 1$): Eq.~\eqref{eq:spclose} and
        $aA + \hat x_1 + \tfrac94\hat x_3 = 19\,cC$;
  \item \emph{Al$_2$O$_3$ only} ($c = 1$): Eq.~\eqref{eq:spclose} and
        $a\hat x_0 + C = b\big(\tfrac23\hat x_1 + \tfrac14\hat x_3\big)$.
\end{itemize}
Each is two linear equations in two unknowns. For the example of Eq.~\eqref{eq:spex}
(FeO only: $A = 0.8$, $B = 0.1$, $C = 0.1$), closure gives $0.8a + 0.1c = 0.9$ and the FeO
condition gives $0.8a + 0.1 = 1.9c$, so $c = 0.5$ and $a = 1.0625$: $\hat x_2$ is halved to
the pure-MgAl$_2$O$_4$ value of 0.05, $\hat x_{\rm Chr}$ and $\hat x_0$ grow by 6\%, and
Al$_2$O$_3$ stays positive.

Setting both oxides to zero when only one is violated is wrong for Al-deficient Fe--Ti
spinels: it forced FeO to zero (in the Bishop Tuff emulator, 4 instead of about 34 wt\%
FeO, and 20 instead of about 1 wt\% MgO).

\paragraph{Both violated.} If both oxides are negative, or if fixing one pushes the other
below zero, all three factors are solved together: closure plus FeO $= 0$ plus
Al$_2$O$_3$ $= 0$,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    A & B & C\\
    A & \hat x_1 + \tfrac94\hat x_3 & -19C\\
    \hat x_0 & -\big(\tfrac23\hat x_1 + \tfrac14\hat x_3\big) & C
  \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix} a\\ b\\ c \end{pmatrix}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix} A + B + C\\ 0\\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Empty pools.} If a pool is empty ($< 10^{-8}$), its factor multiplies zero and
the three-pool system is singular, so these rows are handled separately:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $C = 0$: FeO cannot be negative (no negative FeO term), so only the Al$_2$O$_3$
        case is solved, for $a$ and $b$;
  \item $B = 0$: Al$_2$O$_3$ cannot be negative, so only the FeO case is solved, for $a$
        and $c$;
  \item two or three empty pools: there is nothing to trade between, and the row is left
        unchanged.
\end{itemize}
If the three-pool solve is still singular, an FeO-only correction (\code{polish_negative_spFe})
is applied and the whole correction is retried, up to two times.

\subsection{Where the polish is applied}

The polish is not part of training: the losses of Sections~3 and~6 see the raw softmax
output. It is applied automatically at inference in the default deployment path:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item inside the iterative mass balance of \code{NN_MELTS} (the default for every MELTS
        emulator), after every correction-and-clamp step, for MELTS models that contain
        orthopyroxene, clinopyroxene or spinel;
  \item in the gated model's own forward pass, whatever the mass-balance mode.
\end{itemize}
For \code{ContinuousModel} and \code{AffinityModel} it is \emph{not} applied when the mass
balance is \code{'none'} or \code{'pinv'}.

The mass-balance step moves component moles within a phase and can create a negative oxide
even when the network's own output was legal: on the 102 Closed Cr test set, the iterative
mass balance raises the number of illegal rows from 72 to 469 (orthopyroxene CaO), 0 to 12
(clinopyroxene MgO) and 10 to 134 (spinel FeO, Al$_2$O$_3$). This is why the polish runs
after every step. For each present phase with component moles $m$ and abundance
$n = \sum m$,
\begin{equation}
  \hat x = m / n \ \longrightarrow\ \text{polish (opx, then cpx, then spinel)}
  \ \longrightarrow\ m = n\,\hat x .
\end{equation}
Every polish keeps $\sum \hat x = 1$, so phase abundances are unchanged. The returned
compositions are legal except for spinel rows with two or more empty pools and
clinopyroxene rows with nothing on the positive side, which rescaling cannot correct.

\section{Mass balance}
\label{sec:massbalance}
%=====================================================================================

The assemblage predicted by the emulator does not, in general, reproduce the requested
bulk composition exactly: $\hat b \neq b$. This section describes the correction applied at
inference by \code{MassBalanceProjector} (\code{mass_balance.py}), which
\code{NN_MELTS.apply_mass_balance} calls by default. The correction follows Asimow \&
Ghiorso (1998, \emph{Am.\ Mineral.}\ 83, 1127--1132): the predicted component moles
$\hat m$ are perturbed by the smallest change, measured in relative terms, that makes
$\hat b = b$. Two things differ from their procedure. The minimisation is solved in a form
that corrects thousands of assemblages at once on a GPU, and the correction is repeated a
few times so that no abundance becomes negative.

The section is organised as follows.
Sections~\ref{sec:mb-problem}--\ref{sec:mb-reg} derive a single correction step: the
problem, the criterion that picks one solution, the solution by Lagrange multipliers, its
relation to Asimow and Ghiorso's SVD solution, and the regularisation that keeps it
solvable. Section~\ref{sec:seed} describes a preparatory step for bulk elements that no
predicted phase carries. Section~\ref{sec:mb-iter} assembles the full procedure.

All quantities are those of Table~\ref{tab:key}; Table~\ref{tab:mbkey} lists the symbols
this section adds. As in Section~2, vectors are rows, so $\hat m A$ is a row of E element
moles. Every quantity is for a single row of a batch; the implementation applies the same
operations to all B rows at once. Index forms are given alongside the matrix forms
throughout, with $c$ running over components and $e, e'$ over elements.

\subsection{The problem}
\label{sec:mb-problem}

The network returns the component moles $\hat m$ (the endmembers of every phase, in the
transformed basis of Section~\ref{sec:polish}). The element moles they imply are $\hat m A$
($[1\times\text{C}][\text{C}\times\text{E}] \to [1\times\text{E}]$), and nothing in the
network forces their proportions to equal the requested bulk $b$ (the bulk loss $L_b$ of
Section~3 only encourages it). Only the proportions matter, so the target is the requested
bulk scaled to the prediction's total, $\Gamma\, b$, and the residual is
\begin{equation}
  \delta b = \Gamma\, b - \hat m A = \Gamma\,\big(b - \hat b\big)
  \qquad (\text{E entries}).
  \label{eq:mbr}
\end{equation}
Because $\sum_e b_e = 1$, the entries of $\delta b$ sum to zero: correcting it moves moles
between elements without changing the total. (Asimow and Ghiorso write $\hat m$ as
$m_{\rm sol}$, $A^{\!\top}$ as $T_{\rm sol}$ and $\delta b$ as $dm_{\rm sys}$, and use oxides
in place of elements; the algebra is the same.) Mass balance seeks a correction
$\delta\hat m$ to the component moles such that
\begin{equation}
  \delta\hat m\, A = \delta b,
  \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
  \sum_c \delta\hat m_c\, A_{ce} = \delta b_e \quad \text{for each element } e .
  \label{eq:mbcond}
\end{equation}
The change in the amount of element $e$, summed over every component that contains it,
must equal that element's residual. This is E equations (about ten) in C unknowns (several
tens), so it is underconstrained. There is a $(\text{C} - \text{E})$-dimensional family of
solutions. For example, a deficit in Mg can be made up by adding forsterite, by adding
enstatite, or by adding forsterite and removing an equivalent amount of silica elsewhere.
One member of the family must be chosen.

\subsection{Choosing one solution: the smallest relative change}
\label{sec:mb-objective}

The network's prediction is the best available estimate of the assemblage, so the natural
choice is the correction that changes it least. ``Least'' requires a measure of size.

\paragraph{Unweighted.} The simplest measure is the sum of squared changes,
$\sum_c \delta\hat m_c^2$. Squares are used, rather than absolute values, because they make
the minimisation a linear problem (below), and because they penalise one large change more
than several small ones. This measure treats a trace component and a major one alike,
however: a change of $10^{-3}$ mol is negligible for a phase of 0.5 mol but larger than a
phase of $10^{-4}$ mol.

\paragraph{Weighted (Asimow \& Ghiorso).} To perturb the assemblage least in
\emph{relative} terms, measure each change against the component's own abundance. Define
the relative correction
\begin{equation}
  \zeta_c = \frac{\delta\hat m_c}{\hat m_c},
  \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
  \delta\hat m = \zeta\, \operatorname{diag}(\hat m),
  \label{eq:agback}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{diag}(\hat m)$ is the C$\times$C matrix with the component moles on its
diagonal and zeros elsewhere. (Multiplying a vector by a diagonal matrix scales each entry
by the matching diagonal element, so $(\zeta\operatorname{diag}(\hat m))_c = \hat m_c\,\zeta_c$.)
The quantity to minimise is the sum of squared relative changes,
\begin{equation}
  \tfrac12 \sum_c \zeta_c^2 = \tfrac12\, \zeta\, \zeta^{\!\top}
  = \tfrac12 \sum_c \left(\frac{\delta\hat m_c}{\hat m_c}\right)^{\!2}.
  \label{eq:mbobj}
\end{equation}
The factor $\tfrac12$ only cancels the 2 produced by differentiating a square. Substituting
Eq.~\eqref{eq:agback} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:mbcond} writes the constraint in terms of $\zeta$,
\begin{equation}
  \zeta\, D = \delta b,
  \qquad
  D = \operatorname{diag}(\hat m)\, A \quad (\text{C}\times\text{E}),
  \qquad
  D_{ce} = \hat m_c\, A_{ce} .
  \label{eq:agweighted}
\end{equation}
$D$ is the abundance-weighted stoichiometry: row $c$ is the element content of component
$c$ scaled by that component's moles. A component absent from the assemblage
($\hat m_c = 0$) has a zero row in $D$ and, by Eq.~\eqref{eq:agback}, $\delta\hat m_c = 0$
whatever $\zeta_c$ is, so absent components are never changed.

A single correction step is therefore:
\begin{equation}
  \text{minimise } \tfrac12\, \zeta\,\zeta^{\!\top}
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad \zeta\, D = \delta b .
  \label{eq:mbopt}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Solving with Lagrange multipliers}
\label{sec:mb-lagrange}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:mbopt} is a minimisation subject to equality constraints. The method of
Lagrange multipliers turns it into a set of ordinary equations. One new unknown, the
multiplier $\nu_e$, is introduced for each constraint (here, one per element). Each
constraint, written as an expression that must equal zero, is multiplied by its multiplier
and subtracted from the objective. Requiring every derivative of the result to vanish then
expresses two conditions at once: the constraints hold, and no change that respects them
can lower the objective. The function so formed is the Lagrangian,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\zeta, \nu)
  = \tfrac12 \sum_c \zeta_c^2
    - \sum_e \nu_e \Big( \sum_c \zeta_c\, D_{ce} - \delta b_e \Big)
  = \tfrac12\, \zeta\,\zeta^{\!\top} - \big( \zeta D - \delta b \big)\, \nu^{\!\top},
  \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
with $\nu$ the row of E multipliers. The solution follows in four steps.

\paragraph{Step 1: derivative with respect to $\zeta$.} Differentiating with respect to one
relative correction $\zeta_c$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \zeta_c} = \zeta_c - \sum_e D_{ce}\, \nu_e = 0
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  \zeta = \nu\, D^{\!\top}
  \quad [1\times\text{E}][\text{E}\times\text{C}] \to [1\times\text{C}].
  \label{eq:ystat}
\end{equation}
This is the key result. The C relative corrections are not independent: all of them are set
by just E numbers, the multipliers. The problem has been reduced from C unknowns to E.

\paragraph{Step 2: derivative with respect to $\nu$.} Differentiating with respect to one
multiplier $\nu_e$ gives $-\big(\sum_c \zeta_c\, D_{ce} - \delta b_e\big) = 0$, which is the
constraint $\zeta D = \delta b$ again. This is how the Lagrangian enforces it.

\paragraph{Step 3: solve for the multipliers.} Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:ystat} into the
constraint gives
\begin{equation}
  \nu\, G = \delta b,
  \qquad
  G = D^{\!\top} D = A^{\!\top} \operatorname{diag}(\hat m)^2 A
  \quad [\text{E}\times\text{C}][\text{C}\times\text{C}][\text{C}\times\text{E}] \to [\text{E}\times\text{E}],
  \label{eq:dual}
\end{equation}
with entries
\begin{equation}
  G_{ee'} = \sum_c A_{ce}\, \hat m_c^2\, A_{ce'} .
  \label{eq:Gentries}
\end{equation}
$G_{ee'}$ measures how much elements $e$ and $e'$ are carried by the same present
components, weighted by the square of their moles; the diagonal entry $G_{ee}$ is the total
capacity of the present components to move element $e$. $G$ is square, symmetric and only
E$\times$E (about $10\times10$), however many components the emulator represents. Because
it is symmetric, $\nu G = \delta b$ is the same linear system as $G \nu^{\!\top} = \delta b^{\!\top}$,
which is the form passed to the solver. When $D$ has full column rank, $G$ is invertible and
the system has exactly one solution. Full column rank means that every element is carried
by at least one present component and that no two elements occur in the same fixed
proportion in every present component. Section~\ref{sec:mb-reg} treats the case where this
fails.

\paragraph{Step 4: back-substitute.} With $\nu$ known, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ystat} gives the relative
corrections and Eq.~\eqref{eq:agback} the correction itself. Component by component,
\begin{equation}
  \delta\hat m_c = \hat m_c^2 \sum_e A_{ce}\, \nu_e ,
  \qquad
  \zeta_c = \frac{\delta\hat m_c}{\hat m_c} = \hat m_c \sum_e A_{ce}\, \nu_e .
  \label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
A correction step thus consists of forming the E$\times$E matrix $G$, solving one E$\times$E
linear system, and two matrix products.

\paragraph{How the correction is shared.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:share} shows how the correction is
distributed. Each multiplier $\nu_e$ acts as a ``push'' on element $e$. Each component moves
by the sum of the pushes on its elements, weighted by its stoichiometry $A_{ce}$, and scaled
by its moles squared. The relative change of a component is therefore proportional to its
own abundance. If two components carry the same element and one is 100 times more abundant,
it takes $10^4$ times more of the correction in moles and a 100 times larger relative
change. As Asimow and Ghiorso put it, mass is transferred mostly from the most abundant
phase components, so trace phases keep almost exactly their predicted amounts. Formally,
the multipliers play the role that element chemical potentials play in Gibbs minimisation.
There they are the Lagrange multipliers of the same mass-balance constraints, and each
component's chemical potential is likewise the stoichiometry-weighted sum of its elements'
potentials.

\subsection{Relation to the SVD solution}
\label{sec:mb-svd}

Asimow and Ghiorso obtain the same minimiser by singular value decomposition. Write
\begin{equation}
  D = \Phi\,\operatorname{diag}(\sigma)\,\Psi^{\!\top},
  \qquad \sigma = (\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_{\rm E}),
\end{equation}
with $\Phi$ (C$\times$E) and $\Psi$ (E$\times$E) having orthonormal columns
($\Phi^{\!\top}\Phi = \Psi^{\!\top}\Psi = I$). Geometrically, $D$ rotates, stretches each of E
perpendicular directions by $\sigma_i$, and rotates again. Undoing each stretch gives the
solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mbopt} with the smallest norm,
\begin{equation}
  \zeta = \delta b\; \Psi\,\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^{+})\,\Phi^{\!\top} = \delta b\, D^{+},
  \qquad \sigma_i^{+} = \begin{cases} 1/\sigma_i, & \sigma_i > 0,\\ 0, & \sigma_i = 0, \end{cases}
  \label{eq:agsvd}
\end{equation}
where $D^{+}$ is the Moore--Penrose pseudo-inverse. The Lagrange solution is identical.
Substituting the SVD into Eq.~\eqref{eq:dual} gives
$G = \Psi\operatorname{diag}(\sigma)^2\Psi^{\!\top}$, and so
\begin{equation}
  \zeta = \delta b\, G^{-1} D^{\!\top}
    = \delta b\; \Psi\operatorname{diag}(\sigma)^{-2}\Psi^{\!\top}\,
      \Psi\operatorname{diag}(\sigma)\Phi^{\!\top}
    = \delta b\; \Psi\operatorname{diag}(\sigma)^{-1}\Phi^{\!\top}.
\end{equation}
The two routes differ only in how the same answer is computed. The SVD is the more general
route, because it also handles a $D$ without full column rank (a zero $\sigma_i$ is simply
skipped). Section~\ref{sec:mb-reg} gives the Lagrange route the same ability.

\paragraph{Why the Lagrange form is used.} Asimow and Ghiorso correct one assemblage at a
time, so they can restrict $D$ to the components actually present. That submatrix is dense
and well conditioned. The emulator corrects a batch of B assemblages at once. That requires
a single fixed-shape array spanning all C components of every phase the emulator
represents, with absent components entering as zero rows of $D$. Zero rows do not change
the minimiser, so this is mathematically the same as Asimow and Ghiorso's restriction. It
does make the matrices very sparse, because most components are absent from most
assemblages. In a HeFESTo test bundle (B = 15{,}000, E = 8, C = 68), 94\% of the entries
of $D$ are zero and 74\% of the components are absent from the median row.
% TODO(Ben): confirm the bundle name and replace the timings below with the profiled values.
Batched SVD handles these matrices poorly on a GPU. In a preliminary benchmark on that
bundle, \code{torch.linalg.pinv} took 10.1\,s on the GPU against 0.29\,s on the CPU (single
precision), whereas the Lagrange solve took 25\,ms on the GPU (double precision; 48\,ms on
the CPU). The Lagrange form is unaffected by the sparsity. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:Gentries} the
entries of $G$ are sums over components, to which absent components contribute nothing,
and the system to solve is E$\times$E whatever C is.

\paragraph{The cost: conditioning.} Forming $G = D^{\!\top} D$ squares the condition number,
$\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$, which measures how close a matrix is to singular and hence
how much round-off error a solve amplifies. On the same bundle the condition number of $D$
has median $1.4\times10^{2}$, 99th percentile $5\times10^{4}$ and maximum $10^{10}$, so
that of $G$ reaches $10^{20}$. That is far beyond what single precision resolves (relative
precision $\sim\!10^{-7}$), and in single precision the Lagrange solution departs from the SVD
solution by more than 100\% on the worst-conditioned rows. $G$ is therefore formed and solved
in double precision (relative precision $\sim\!10^{-16}$), and the correction is formed in
double precision before being cast back.

\subsection{Regularisation}
\label{sec:mb-reg}

\paragraph{Why it is needed.} $G$ is singular, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:dual} cannot be solved,
whenever an element has no carrier among the present components. Its column of $D$ is then
zero, and so are its row and column of $G$. This is common: most rows contain an element
absent from both the bulk and every present component, for example Cr in a Cr-free bulk
(13{,}254 of 15{,}000 rows of the 102 Closed Cr test set).

\paragraph{The regularised solve.} A small number $\kappa$ is added to every diagonal entry
of $G$,
\begin{equation}
  \nu\,\big(G + \kappa I\big) = \delta b,
  \qquad \kappa = 10^{-12},
  \label{eq:regsolve}
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the E$\times$E identity matrix (ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere). For an
element with no carrier, its equation becomes $\kappa\,\nu_e = \delta b_e$, which is
solvable. Substituting the SVD as before gives
\begin{equation}
  \zeta = \delta b\; \Psi\, \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2 + \kappa}\right) \Phi^{\!\top}.
  \label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
This is Eq.~\eqref{eq:agsvd} with each $1/\sigma_i$ replaced by $\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2 + \kappa)$.
For $\sigma_i^2 \gg \kappa$ the two are the same. For $\sigma_i = 0$ both give zero, so
elements with no carrier are ignored rather than making the solve fail. The regularisation
is therefore a smooth version of the pseudo-inverse's rule of skipping zero singular values.
For small but real $\sigma_i$, those of elements carried only by trace phases, the factor
$\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2 + \kappa)$ shrinks the step whenever $\kappa$ is comparable to
$\sigma_i^2$.

\paragraph{Choice of $\kappa$.} On the 102 test set the smallest non-zero diagonal entry of
$G$ is typically $3\times10^{-7}$ (median over rows), so $\kappa$ must be far smaller than
that. With $\kappa = 10^{-12}$ the filter is indistinguishable from $1/\sigma_i$ for every
element that has a carrier, and the regularised solve reproduces the SVD solution. With the
iteration of Section~\ref{sec:mb-iter} otherwise unchanged, the median final residual on
that test set is $3.7\times10^{-5}$ for the earlier value $\kappa = 10^{-6}$,
$3.0\times10^{-8}$ for $\kappa = 10^{-12}$, and $2.0\times10^{-8}$ for the exact SVD
solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:agsvd}. $\kappa = 0$ is not possible, because of the exactly
singular rows above. A $\kappa$ this small also requires double precision: diagonal entries
of $G$ reach order ten, and in single precision adding $10^{-12}$ to them is lost to
round-off.

\paragraph{$\kappa$ does not create phases.} The regularisation is added to the
E$\times$E element system, not to the component moles. It changes the multipliers $\nu$,
never the weights. An absent component has $\hat m_c = 0$, so its correction
$\delta\hat m_c = \hat m_c^2 \sum_e A_{ce}\,\nu_e$ is exactly zero whatever $\nu$ is. The
same holds for an element with no carrier. Its column of $D$ is zero, so $\nu_e$ is
multiplied by zero in every present component, and its residual (zero when the element is
absent from the bulk) is left alone. On the 102 test set, no component that was zero before
the correction (after seeding) is non-zero after it.

\subsection{Seeding phases for unexplained elements}
\label{sec:seed}

\paragraph{Why it is needed.} The correction can only move an element through components
that contain it and are present ($\hat m_c > 0$). If the predicted assemblage contains no
carrier of an element that is in the bulk (for example K, P or H when the network places
the K-, P- or H-bearing phase just below saturation), that element's residual cannot be
removed: the regularised solve leaves it in place (Section~\ref{sec:mb-reg}). In the 102
Closed Cr test set this happens in 13 of 15{,}000 rows.

\paragraph{Procedure.} Before the correction, for the continuous models
(\code{NN_MELTS.seed_unexplained_phases}), repeat for each row:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Unexplained elements.} Element $e$ is unexplained if $b_e > 0$ and no
        component with $\hat m_c > 0$ contains it ($A_{ce} = 0$ for every present $c$). Call
        the set of unexplained elements $\mathcal{U}$.
  \item \textbf{Candidates.} Absent phases ($\hat n_p = 0$) whose \emph{predicted}
        composition contains at least one element of $\mathcal{U}$. The chemistry heads
        predict a composition $\hat x$ for every phase, present or not, so one is always
        available.
  \item \textbf{Choice.} The candidate closest to saturation: the largest (least negative)
        affinity $g_p$ ($\hat g_p$ for \code{AffinityModel}).
  \item \textbf{Abundance.} Let
        \begin{equation}
          a_e = \sum_c M_{pc}\,(\hat x V + f)_c\, A_{ce}
          \label{eq:seedcomp}
        \end{equation}
        be the moles of element $e$ in one mole of the chosen phase $p$: its predicted
        component fractions (Eq.~\ref{eq:m}, restricted to phase $p$ by $M_{pc}$) times
        their stoichiometry. The phase is added with the abundance $\hat n_p$ that best
        supplies the elements of $\mathcal{U}$ it carries,
        \begin{equation}
          \hat n_p = \frac{\sum_{e \in \mathcal{U}} a_e\, \Gamma\, b_e}{\sum_{e \in \mathcal{U}} a_e^2},
          \label{eq:seed}
        \end{equation}
        the least-squares fit of $\hat n_p\, a_e = \Gamma\, b_e$ over those elements. When the
        phase covers a single unexplained element, $\hat n_p$ supplies exactly the bulk's
        amount of it, $\hat n_p = \Gamma\, b_e / a_e$.
  \item \textbf{Insert.} The phase's components get $\hat m_c = \hat n_p\,(\hat x V + f)_c$
        for every $c$ with $M_{pc} = 1$, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:m}.
\end{enumerate}
The loop stops when $\mathcal{U}$ is empty or no candidate is left; each round adds a new
phase, so it ends after at most P rounds. $\hat m$ is then renormalised so that
$\Gamma = 1$. Rows that need no seeding are not touched.

\paragraph{Effect.} Seeding gives every element of the bulk a non-zero column in $D$.
Because the seeded phase already carries about the right amount of the missing element, it
also gets a sensible weight: the correction moves each component in proportion to
$\hat m_c^2$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:share}), so a phase added at a negligible abundance would barely
be used. On the 102 test set, 14 phases were seeded in the 13 rows, and 11 of them are
present in the ground truth (apatite, leucite, fluid, hornblende). A single unclamped
correction step on those rows reduces their residuals from order $10^{-3}$ to about
$10^{-8}$.

\subsection{The iterative correction}
\label{sec:mb-iter}

\paragraph{Why one step is not enough.} The step of Sections~\ref{sec:mb-lagrange}--\ref{sec:mb-reg}
places no bound on $\delta\hat m_c$. Nothing prevents $\delta\hat m_c < -\hat m_c$, which
would leave a component with negative moles; this is the ``overstepping'' failure Asimow
and Ghiorso note. Setting such a component to zero restores a physical assemblage but
breaks the mass balance again, because the moles it would have supplied are now missing.
The step is therefore repeated: each repetition spreads the remaining residual over the
components that are still present.

\paragraph{Procedure.} Starting from the seeded $\hat m$, each of $N_{\rm it}$ iterations
($N_{\rm it} = 3$ by default) does:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Residual on the current scale.} Recompute $\Gamma = \sum_e (\hat m A)_e$ and
        $\delta b = \Gamma\, b - \hat m A$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:mbr}). Only the proportions of the
        bulk are enforced, and each clamp (step 4) changes the total, so the target
        $\Gamma\, b$ is rebuilt every iteration. A target held at a fixed scale would make
        the residual grow between iterations.
  \item \textbf{Weights.} Build $D = \operatorname{diag}(\hat m)\,A$, with each present
        $\hat m_c$ raised to at least $10^{-9}$ in the diagonal. The floor keeps a very small
        but present component solvable; absent components keep a zero row.
  \item \textbf{Solve.} Form $G = D^{\!\top} D$, solve the regularised system
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:regsolve} for $\nu$, then $\zeta = \nu D^{\!\top}$
        (Eq.~\ref{eq:ystat}), all in double precision. This is the operation that runs
        batched on the GPU.
  \item \textbf{Update and clamp.} $\hat m \leftarrow \max\big(\hat m + \zeta \operatorname{diag}(\hat m),\ 0\big)$,
        element by element: Eq.~\eqref{eq:agback}, followed by setting any negative
        component moles to zero.
  \item \textbf{Polish.} For MELTS models containing orthopyroxene, clinopyroxene or spinel,
        re-impose the legality corrections of Section~\ref{sec:polish}. The update moves
        component moles within a phase and can create a negative oxide in a phase whose
        predicted composition was legal.
\end{enumerate}
After the last iteration the remaining residual, $\lVert \hat b - b \rVert$ with $\hat b$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bhat}, is reported for each row.

\paragraph{Properties.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Termination.} A component set to zero has a zero row in $D$ from then on and
        cannot return. The set of present components can only shrink, so the iteration
        cannot cycle, and a phase removed by the correction stays removed.
  \item \emph{Exactness.} Each unclamped step removes the residual up to the
        regularisation. After the final clamp and polish a small residual can remain: the
        median on the 102 test set is $3.0\times10^{-8}$.
  \item \emph{Non-negativity and legality.} Returned component moles are never negative,
        and the polish runs after every step rather than only at the end.
  \item \emph{Special cases.} With one iteration, no clamp, no polish and $\kappa \to 0$,
        the procedure reduces to the single weighted step of Asimow and Ghiorso,
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:agsvd}. That single step remains available for comparison as
        \code{mass_balance='pinv'}, evaluated by SVD on the CPU. Setting
        \code{relative=False} replaces $\operatorname{diag}(\hat m)$ by the identity on
        present components, giving the unweighted smallest change.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Summary}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{7.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & Code argument & Default & Role \\
\midrule
$N_{\rm it}$ & \code{iters} & 3 & Correction--clamp--polish iterations \\
$\kappa$ & \code{tikhonov} & $10^{-12}$ & Regularisation of the E$\times$E solve, Eq.~\eqref{eq:regsolve} \\
-- & \code{weight_floor} & $10^{-9}$ & Smallest weight given to a present component \\
-- & \code{damping} & 1 & Fraction of each step applied ($\hat m \leftarrow \hat m + \text{damping}\cdot \delta\hat m$) \\
-- & \code{relative} & \code{True} & Relative (Asimow--Ghiorso) or unweighted objective \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In short, each step finds the smallest relative change to the predicted component moles
$\hat m$ that restores $\hat b = b$. The step is solved through one multiplier per element,
which gives the same answer as Asimow and Ghiorso's SVD while working on sparse, batched
inputs on a GPU. A tiny regularisation keeps the solve defined when an element has no
carrier. Repeating the step with a clamp and the legality polish yields component moles that
are non-negative, legal and mass-balanced to about $10^{-8}$.
